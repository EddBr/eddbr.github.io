Regret

Regret is the reward an agent gives up by not pulling the best arm every time. It turns "explores well" into a number you can plot, and it separates the bandit algorithms from Lessons 5 to 9 by how fast that number grows.

The race

Greedy, ε-greedy and UCB (Lesson 9) on 300 testbed bandits with 10 arms, all with sample-average estimates starting at 0. The curve is expected regret: the sum of the gaps of the arms actually pulled, averaged over bandits.

The maths

Let $a^\star$ be the best arm. The gap of arm $a$ is how much worse it is on average:

$$\Delta_a = q_*(a^\star) - q_*(a) \ge 0$$

An oracle that always pulls $a^\star$ earns $T\,q_*(a^\star)$ in expectation over $T$ steps. The regret is the shortfall:

$$\text{Regret}_T = T\,q_*(a^\star) - \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T} q_*(A_t)\Big]$$

It uses $q_*(A_t)$, not the noisy reward $R_t$, so good or bad luck on a pull does not count. Only the choice does.

Let $N_T(a)$ be how often arm $a$ is pulled in $T$ steps. Grouping the sum by arm, $\sum_t q_*(A_t) = \sum_a N_T(a)\,q_*(a)$, and $T = \sum_a N_T(a)$. So

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Regret}_T &= \sum_a \mathbb{E}[N_T(a)]\,q_*(a^\star) - \sum_a \mathbb{E}[N_T(a)]\,q_*(a) \\ &= \sum_a \Delta_a\, \mathbb{E}[N_T(a)] \end{aligned}$$

Regret is "how often you pulled each bad arm, times how bad it is". That is the table in the interactive.

ε-greedy is linear. On every step it explores with probability $\varepsilon$ and picks one of the $k$ arms uniformly, so $\mathbb{E}[N_T(a)] \ge \varepsilon T / k$ for every arm. Then

$$\text{Regret}_T \ge \frac{\varepsilon}{k}\Big(\sum_a \Delta_a\Big)\, T$$

It keeps paying for exploration at the same rate forever. Greedy is also linear: with some probability it locks onto a wrong arm and never leaves.

UCB is logarithmic. A bad arm's bonus $c\sqrt{\ln t / N_t(a)}$ only beats the gap $\Delta_a$ while $N_t(a)$ is below roughly $c^2 \ln t / \Delta_a^2$. So each bad arm is pulled about $\ln T / \Delta_a^2$ times (up to a constant), and the regret grows like $\ln T$.

No algorithm can do better than logarithmic. Lai and Robbins (1985) showed that for any algorithm that does well on every bandit, with unit-variance Gaussian rewards,

$$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{\text{Regret}_T}{\ln T} \ge \sum_{a:\,\Delta_a > 0} \frac{2}{\Delta_a}$$

Arms that are nearly as good as the best are the hardest: telling them apart takes many pulls.
