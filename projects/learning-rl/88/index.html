The KL penalty

A reward model is only accurate on the kind of replies it was trained on. Optimise against it without limits and the policy finds replies it scores wrongly. RLHF subtracts $\beta$ times the KL divergence from the original model, which keeps the policy close to where the reward model can be trusted.

The penalty, one sample at a time

In training the KL is not summed over every reply. Each sampled reply gets the reward $r(y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$, and the average of that is the objective (Lesson 81's sample estimate).

The maths

With $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ the model before RL (usually the supervised fine-tuned one) and $r_\phi$ the reward model from Lesson 87, RLHF maximises

$$\begin{aligned} J(\pi) = \;&\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x, y)\big] \\ &- \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The first term asks for high reward. The second charges for every nat of distance from the reference model. $\beta$ sets the exchange rate.

The exact optimum. Fix $x$ and write $Z = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} J(\pi) &= \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[r(y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big] \\ &= -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}}\Big] \\ &= -\beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*\big) + \beta \log Z, \\ \pi^*(y) &= \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}}{Z} \end{aligned}$$

$\beta \log Z$ does not depend on $\pi$, and KL is zero only when the two distributions match (Lesson 81). So the best policy is $\pi^*$: the reference model reweighted by $e^{r/\beta}$. The green bars are $\pi^*$, and the line under the stats checks that $J(\pi^*) = \beta \log Z$.

As $\beta \to \infty$, $\pi^* \to \pi_{\text{ref}}$. As $\beta \to 0$, $\pi^*$ piles onto the highest $r$, flaw or not. This is Lesson 84's calculation with $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in place of the uniform policy.

Per token. Because $\log \pi(y \mid x) = \sum_t \log \pi(y_t \mid s_t)$ (Lesson 85), the sequence penalty splits into a penalty on each token. Ouyang et al. (2022, eq. 2) write the objective with the log ratio of the whole reply; PPO implementations split it and give the policy the per-token reward

$$\begin{aligned} R_t = &-\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t)} \\ &+ \mathbb{1}_{t = T}\, r_\phi(x, y) \end{aligned}$$

The sampled log ratio is an unbiased estimate of the KL. A single sample can be negative; its expectation cannot.

Overoptimisation. The curve above has the shape Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured on real reward models: as KL from the reference grows, the reward model's score keeps rising while true quality peaks and then falls. That is Lesson 91's topic.
