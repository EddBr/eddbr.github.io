The log-derivative trick

The policy gradient is a sum over all actions, but an agent only sees the action it sampled. Dividing that action's gradient by its probability fixes the bookkeeping: a rare action turns up rarely, so each time it does, it must push harder. $\nabla \pi / \pi$ is exactly $\nabla \ln \pi$.

Rare actions push harder

Two actions, one parameter: $\pi(\rightarrow) = \sigma(\theta) = 1/(1 + e^{-\theta})$ and $\pi(\leftarrow) = 1 - \sigma(\theta)$. Drag $\theta$.

Green: $\pi(\rightarrow \mid \theta)$. Purple: $\ln \pi(\rightarrow \mid \theta)$. Dashed lines are tangents at your $\theta$. The log curve's slope is the green curve's slope divided by its height.

Columns: how often the action is sampled, how hard one sample pushes $\theta$, and the product. The product is $\nabla_\theta \pi(a)$ for both actions, whatever $\theta$ is. Push $\theta$ far left: right becomes rare, and its push grows towards 1 while its frequency shrinks.

The maths

The chain rule applied to $\ln$ gives the identity that names this lesson:

$$\nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s, \boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{\nabla \pi(a \mid s, \boldsymbol\theta)}{\pi(a \mid s, \boldsymbol\theta)}$$

The gradient of the log is the gradient divided by the probability.

Why it is needed. The gradient of an expectation over actions is a weighted sum of $\nabla\pi$, not an expectation. Multiplying and dividing by $\pi$ turns it back into one:

$$\nabla\, \mathbb{E}_{A \sim \pi}[f(A)] = \sum_a f(a)\, \nabla \pi(a) = \sum_a \pi(a)\, f(a)\, \frac{\nabla \pi(a)}{\pi(a)} = \mathbb{E}_{A \sim \pi}\big[f(A)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A)\big]$$

Here $f$ must not depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$. The last form is an average over sampled actions, so one sample $f(A)\nabla\ln\pi(A)$ is an unbiased estimate. Without the $1/\pi$, each action's term is weighted by how often it is sampled as well, which is the wrong estimator in the second chart.

The softmax case. With linear preferences $h(s,a,\boldsymbol\theta) = \boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}(s,a)$ (Lesson 70), $\ln \pi(a \mid s) = \boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}(s,a) - \ln \sum_b e^{\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}(s,b)}$, so

$$\nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s, \boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf{x}(s,a) - \sum_b \pi(b \mid s, \boldsymbol\theta)\, \mathbf{x}(s,b)$$

This is S&B eq. 13.9: the action's features minus the average features. For the one-parameter policy above it gives $1 - \pi(\rightarrow)$ for right and $-\pi(\rightarrow)$ for left.

Whole trajectories. The probability of a trajectory $\tau = (S_0, A_0, S_1, A_1, \dots, S_T)$ is

$$P(\tau \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = p(S_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \boldsymbol\theta)\, p(S_{t+1} \mid S_t, A_t)$$

The log turns the product into a sum, and the dynamics terms do not depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$, so their gradients are zero:

$$\nabla \ln P(\tau \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \boldsymbol\theta)$$

With $f(\tau) = G(\tau)$, the return of the trajectory, the same trick gives a gradient the agent can estimate without knowing $p$:

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau}\Big[G(\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \boldsymbol\theta)\Big]$$
