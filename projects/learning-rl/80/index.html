Why big steps break things

A policy gradient step trusts the slope at the current policy. The slope only holds nearby, and in RL the next data comes from the policy the step produces. One step too far can push a policy somewhere it never collects the data it needs to come back.

The maths

Policy gradient methods (Lessons 70 to 79) do gradient ascent on the expected return $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$ with an estimate $\hat g$ of its gradient:

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_k + \alpha\,\hat g_k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat g_k] = \nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_k)$$

Each step moves the parameters a distance proportional to $\alpha$ along the estimated uphill direction.

The slope is local. By Taylor's theorem, for a step $\Delta$,

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta + \Delta) = J(\boldsymbol\theta) + \nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)^{\top}\Delta + O(\lVert\Delta\rVert^2)$$

The gradient predicts the gain only while the squared term is small. A large $\Delta$ can land anywhere, including downhill.

The step is in the wrong units. For a softmax policy $\pi(a) = e^{\theta_a} / \sum_b e^{\theta_b}$,

$$\frac{\partial \pi(a)}{\partial \theta_a} = \pi(a)\big(1 - \pi(a)\big)$$

Near $\pi(a) = 0.5$ a unit change in $\theta_a$ moves the probability by about $0.25$. Near $\pi(a) = 0.999$ it moves it by about $0.001$. The same $\alpha$ is a huge step in one place and a tiny one in another, which is what the second plot shows.

The data depends on the step. In supervised learning a bad step is fixed by the next batch, which comes from the same dataset. Here the next batch is sampled from $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1}}$. After one noisy reward and a big $\alpha$, a worse arm can reach $\pi \approx 0.999$. The other arms are then almost never tried, and the update on them is scaled by $1 - \pi \approx 0.001$. The run is stuck: these are the red lines.

The fix: limit the step in policy space. Plain gradient ascent already solves a constrained problem. The best linear gain inside a ball of radius $\epsilon$ around $\boldsymbol\theta$ is

$$\max_{\lVert\Delta\rVert \le \epsilon} \nabla J^{\top}\Delta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta = \epsilon\,\frac{\nabla J}{\lVert \nabla J\rVert}$$

so the gradient step is a trust region in parameter space, measured with Euclidean distance. TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) keeps the idea but measures distance between the old and new policies instead of their parameters, with the KL divergence of Lesson 81. PPO (Lessons 82 and 83) gets a similar effect more cheaply by clipping.
