Thompson sampling

Keep a belief curve for each arm, as in Lesson 11. On every step, draw one random guess from each curve and play the arm with the highest guess. Uncertain arms produce wild guesses and get tried. Arms known to be bad almost never win the draw.

Thompson vs UCB vs ε-greedy

Regret (Lesson 10) on 200 bandits with 10 win-or-lose arms, each $\theta_a$ drawn uniformly from $[0, 1]$. UCB here uses the bonus $\sqrt{2 \ln t / N(a)}$, the standard choice for rewards in $[0, 1]$.

The maths

Each arm $a$ has belief $\text{Beta}(\alpha_a, \beta_a)$, starting at $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$. On step $t$:

$$\tilde\theta_a \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_a, \beta_a) \ \text{for each } a$$ $$A_t = \arg\max_a \tilde\theta_a$$

Each arm draws a plausible win rate, and the arm with the best draw is played. Then the reward $R_t \in \{0, 1\}$ updates only that arm, by Lesson 11's counting rule:

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_{A_t} &\leftarrow \alpha_{A_t} + R_t \\ \beta_{A_t} &\leftarrow \beta_{A_t} + (1 - R_t) \end{aligned}$$

A win adds to $\alpha$, a loss adds to $\beta$.

This is probability matching. The chance of playing an arm equals the current belief that it is the best one:

$$\begin{aligned} &P(A_t = a \mid \text{history}) \\ &\quad = P\big(\theta_a = \max_b \theta_b \;\big|\; \text{history}\big) \end{aligned}$$

Drawing one sample from each curve and taking the argmax is a single draw from exactly this distribution, with no need to compute it.

Compare UCB (Lesson 9). UCB acts on the most optimistic plausible value, deterministically. Thompson sampling acts on a random plausible value. Both explore an arm only while it might still be the best. Both have regret that grows like $\ln T$.

Lesson 10's Lai–Robbins term $2/\Delta_a$ is $\Delta_a / (\Delta_a^2/2)$, and $\Delta_a^2/2$ is the KL divergence (Lesson 81) between two unit-variance Gaussians. For win-or-lose arms the KL divergence $d$ between two coins takes its place:

$$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{\text{Regret}_T}{\ln T} \ge \sum_{a:\,\Delta_a > 0} \frac{\Delta_a}{d(\theta_a, \theta^\star)}$$

Here $d(p, q) = p \ln \frac{p}{q} + (1-p) \ln \frac{1-p}{1-q}$ and $\theta^\star$ is the best arm's win rate. Thompson sampling with $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ priors meets this bound exactly (Kaufmann, Korda & Munos, 2012): no algorithm that does well on every bandit has a smaller constant in front of $\ln T$.
