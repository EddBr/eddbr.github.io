Stationary distributions

In Lesson 16, every starting day led to the same distribution after a couple of weeks. That limit is the stationary distribution $\mu$: the distribution that one more step of the chain leaves unchanged. It is also the share of time the chain spends in each state in the long run.

Two states

A town with only sun and rain. $a$ is the chance a sunny day is followed by rain, $b$ the chance a rainy day is followed by sun.

The maths

A distribution $\mu$ over states (a row vector) is stationary if one step of the chain leaves it unchanged:

$$\mu = \mu P, \qquad \sum_s \mu(s) = 1$$

If today's weather is drawn from $\mu$, so is tomorrow's, and every day after.

Two states. Write $x_t$ for $P(\text{sun on day } t)$. A sunny day stays sunny with probability $1 - a$, and a rainy day turns sunny with probability $b$:

$$x_{t+1} = (1 - a)\,x_t + b\,(1 - x_t) = b + (1 - a - b)\,x_t$$

The stationary value is the fixed point, where $x_{t+1} = x_t$. Equivalently, the flow from sun to rain must equal the flow back, $\mu(\text{sun})\,a = \mu(\text{rain})\,b$:

$$\mu(\text{sun}) = \frac{b}{a + b}, \qquad \mu(\text{rain}) = \frac{a}{a + b}$$

How fast. Subtract $\mu(\text{sun}) = b + (1 - a - b)\,\mu(\text{sun})$ from the update, then repeat $t$ times:

$$x_{t+1} - \mu(\text{sun}) = (1 - a - b)\,\big(x_t - \mu(\text{sun})\big)$$ $$x_t - \mu(\text{sun}) = (1 - a - b)^t\,\big(x_0 - \mu(\text{sun})\big)$$

The gap shrinks by a factor $|1 - a - b|$ every day. When $1 - a - b < 0$ it also flips sign, so the curves zig-zag in. With $a = b = 1$ the factor is $-1$: the chain alternates forever and $x_t$ never settles, although the long-run share of sunny days is still $\tfrac{1}{2}$. With $a = b = 0$ each state is a trap and every distribution is stationary.

The general result. A finite chain is irreducible if every state can reach every other, and aperiodic if it does not cycle with a fixed period. If it is both, then $\mu$ is unique, $\mu_0 P^t \to \mu$ from every start, and the fraction of the first $T$ days spent in $s$ tends to $\mu(s)$. In linear-algebra terms, $\mu$ is a left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1.

RL uses this constantly. A fixed policy in an MDP makes a Markov chain over states. Its stationary distribution says how often the agent visits each state, which later sets how much each state's error counts when values are approximated (S&B §9.2).
