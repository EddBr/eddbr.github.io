Blackjack

Blackjack is easy to simulate and awkward to write a model for. That makes it the textbook case for Monte Carlo: play many hands, average the outcomes, and the value of every state appears without ever computing $p(s', r \mid s, a)$.

Play a hand

Get closer to 21 than the dealer without going over. Face cards are 10. An ace is 11 unless that would bust you, then 1. An ace counted as 11 is a usable ace. The dealer hits until 17 or more. Below 12 you can't bust, so you hit automatically.

The maths

The state is what matters for your decision:

$$S = (\text{your sum} \in \{12, \dots, 21\},\ \text{dealer's card} \in \{A, 2, \dots, 10\},\ \text{usable ace} \in \{\text{yes}, \text{no}\})$$

That is $10 \times 10 \times 2 = 200$ states. The deck is infinite (cards drawn with replacement), so the cards already seen don't matter. Sums below 12 are left out because hitting is always right there. A natural (21 on the deal) wins at once unless the dealer also has one, and involves no decision, so the maps leave it out too.

Rewards are $0$ during the hand and $+1$, $0$ or $-1$ at the end, with $\gamma = 1$. So every state in a hand has the same return, the final reward:

$$G_t = R_T \quad \text{for every } t, \qquad V(s) = \frac{1}{N(s)} \sum_{i=1}^{N(s)} R_T^{(i)}$$

The value of a state is your expected winnings from it. First-visit and every-visit (Lesson 36) agree here, because no state can repeat within a hand. A hit either raises your sum and keeps the ace status, or turns a usable ace into a non-usable one (soft 18 plus a 9 is hard 17). A hard hand of 12 or more can never become soft again.

DP could solve this too, but it would need $p(s', r \mid s, a)$, which includes the distribution of the dealer's final total given their up card. Monte Carlo needs only a simulator. As S&B Figure 5.1 shows, the usable-ace map is noisier after 10,000 hands, since usable aces are rarer and those states get fewer samples.
