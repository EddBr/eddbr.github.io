Direct preference optimisation

Lessons 87 and 88 fit a reward model, then run RL against it with a KL penalty. Lesson 88 also found the exact answer to that RL problem. DPO inverts that answer: it trains the policy directly on preference pairs with a classification loss. No reward model and no sampling during training.

The amber circles come from the two-stage route: fit Bradley–Terry scores to the same 40 pairs (Lesson 87), then reweight $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by $e^{r/\beta}$ (Lesson 88). Both routes take the same number of gradient steps on the same pairs, and after every step the green bars sit on the circles.

The maths

Step 1: solve for the reward. Lesson 88 showed the KL-penalised optimum is $\pi^*(y \mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta} / Z(x)$. Take logs and rearrange:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

Any policy defines a reward this way. $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ is called its implicit reward, written $\hat r_\theta$ below.

Step 2: put it into Bradley–Terry. Bradley–Terry (Lesson 86) uses only reward differences, and both replies share the prompt $x$, so $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels:

$$\begin{aligned} P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\!\Big(&\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} \\ &- \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

The intractable $Z$ is gone. The preference probability is written in terms of the policy alone.

Step 3: maximum likelihood. Fit $\pi_\theta$ to the comparisons exactly as Lesson 87 fit $r_\phi$ (Rafailov et al. 2023, eq. 7):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(&\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} \\ &- \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\Big)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

It is Lesson 87's loss with $r_\phi$ replaced by the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$. That is why the two routes land in the same place: they minimise the same function of the rewards.

The gradient. With $\hat r_\theta(y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$,

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}\Big[&\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(y_l) - \hat r_\theta(y_w)\big) \\ &\times \big(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)\big)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

Raise the chosen reply's log-probability, lower the rejected one's, weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward currently orders them. A pair already ordered with a wide margin barely moves anything.

The policy is a softmax over the six whole replies with logits $\theta_y$, so $\log \pi_\theta(y_w) - \log \pi_\theta(y_l) = \theta_w - \theta_l$: the normaliser cancels and each step changes only the two logits in the pair. Replies that never appear in the data keep their logits and change only through the normaliser, so DPO learns nothing about them. The reply that invents a statistic is almost never produced by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, so it shows up in few pairs or none, and what DPO does to it depends on luck. RLHF with a learned reward model would instead score it by extrapolating, as in Lessons 87 and 88.
