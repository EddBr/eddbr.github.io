Tic-tac-toe: learning by self-play

Lesson 42's agent learned from you, one slow game at a time. Here two copies of it, one playing X and one playing O, play each other tens of thousands of times. Neither is told the rules of good play. Within about 30,000 games, both usually stop losing to a perfect player.

Every 1,000 self-play games, each table plays 200 test games greedily ($\varepsilon = 0$, no learning) against a perfect player that picks at random among its best moves. The chart shows the share of those games lost. A perfect player can never be beaten, so 0% lost means only draws.

The maths

Each player $p \in \{\text{X}, \text{O}\}$ keeps its own table $V_p$, scored from its own side: $1$ for its win, $\tfrac12$ for a draw, $0$ for its loss. After a greedy move from its board $S_t$ to its next board $S_{t+1}$, it applies Lesson 42's update:

$$V_p(S_t) \leftarrow V_p(S_t) + \alpha\big[V_p(S_{t+1}) - V_p(S_t)\big]$$

Each table learns from the boards that followed its own moves, and the other player's moves happen in between.

When the game ends, the player who did not make the last move updates its last board towards the final score:

$$V_p(S_\text{last}) \leftarrow V_p(S_\text{last}) + \alpha\big[z_p - V_p(S_\text{last})\big], \qquad z_p \in \{0, \tfrac12\}$$

A loss pulls the board that allowed it towards $0$; a draw pulls it towards $\tfrac12$. The player who made the last move needs no extra rule: its ordinary update already targets the finished board, whose value is fixed at $1$ or $\tfrac12$.

Each player's opponent is the other learner, so the opponent keeps changing. That makes the problem non-stationary, which is why a constant $\alpha$ (Lesson 7) suits it better than a $1/n$ average. It also means the curves can jump: when one table changes, the other's old values can stop being right.

Tic-tac-toe is a draw under perfect play: the minimax value of the empty board is a draw. So the best possible result against the perfect player is 0% lost. Getting there needs exploration ($\varepsilon$ too small and some lines are never tried), and O usually takes longer because it must learn a defence against every opening X tries.
