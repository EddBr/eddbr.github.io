    The five classifiers

    Each classifier learned a different idea of "most". Three of them share one backbone that was trained to resist adversarial attacks, which turns out to matter a lot for how readable the pictures are.

    Why it isn't just noise

    Changing raw pixels gives every spatial frequency the same weight, and the fastest way to raise a score is high-frequency static. So the image is stored as Fourier coefficients scaled by 1/frequency, with colours decorrelated. Smooth shapes become cheap to make and static becomes expensive.

    Why robust models draw better

    An ordinary classifier can be pushed to 100% by patterns no human would recognise. The ImageNet backbone here was adversarially trained (ℓ2, ε = 3), so small meaningless changes can't move it. The only way up is to draw something that actually looks like the thing. Compare Objects with Scenes, which uses a normally trained network, and see the lighthouse specimen with jitter turned off.

    Most and least beautiful look the same

    NIMA predicts how 1–10 photo ratings would be distributed. Push it up and you get pastel swirl rated 8.7, higher than most real photos get. Push it down and you get nearly the same swirl rated 2.6. The difference lives in textures too fine for people to notice. The model reacts to them, but it has no idea what a beautiful scene is.
